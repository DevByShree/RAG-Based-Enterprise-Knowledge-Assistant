Unit 1. Introduction to Software Testing 
What is Software Testing? 
• Software Testing is a systematic process of evaluating a software application 
to ensure it works correctly, meets its specified requirements, and is free of 
defects or security vulnerabilities. The primary goal is to provide objective, 
independent information about the software's quality and to mitigate the risk of 
failure. 
 
•Software Testing involves the implementation of a module or part of a system of 
a product to evaluate all or some of its properties. 
 
• It Improves quality, reliability, and performance 
---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 
Need for Software Testing 
1. To Detect Defects Early 
Testing helps identify errors, faults, and bugs in the software at an early stage, 
reducing the cost and effort required to fix them later. 
2. To Ensure Quality and Reliability 
Software testing ensures that the application performs reliably under expected 
conditions and delivers a high-quality product to users. 
3. To Verify Requirements 
Testing confirms that the software meets specified functional and non-functional 
requirements as defined by the client or user. 
4. To Improve Security and Safety 
Testing helps detect vulnerabilities, failures, and risks that could lead to security 
breaches or system crashes. 
5. To Reduce Maintenance Cost 
Fixing defects during the testing phase is cheaper than correcting them after 
deployment, thereby lowering long-term maintenance costs. 
6. To Enhance Customer Satisfaction 
Well-tested software provides a smooth user experience, increasing customer 
confidence and satisfaction. 
---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 
Evolution of Software Testing 
1. Debugging-Oriented (Early Days): Testing was informal, tied to debugging, focusing 
on removing errors found by programmers after development. 
2. Destruction-Oriented (1970s): A shift to finding ways to break the code and uncover 
defects, a more proactive error-finding phase. 
3. Evaluation-Oriented (1980s): Testing became a measurement of quality, moving 
beyond just breaking code to assessing software quality, with standards like IEEE 829 
emerging. 


--- Page 2 ---

4. Prevention-Oriented & Quality Assurance (1990s-2000s): Focus expanded to 
influencing development processes for better quality (QA) and continuous feedback, 
integrating testing earlier. 
5. Automation & Agile Era (2000s-2010s): Adoption of Agile, XP, DevOps, and CI/CD 
accelerated testing, making it continuous and automated to keep pace with rapid 
releases. 
6. Modern & AI-Driven Era (2010s-Present): Embracing cloud, AI, machine learning, 
and specialized tools for predictive testing, intelligent automation, and monitoring in 
production (Shift-Right), enabling faster, more complex software delivery. 
---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 
 
Objectives of Software Testing 
 
1. To Detect Defects 
Identify errors, bugs, and defects in the software before it is released to users. 
2. To Verify Requirements 
Ensure that the software functions according to functional and non-functional 
requirements. 
3. To Validate the Product 
Confirm that the developed software satisfies user needs and business expectations. 
4. To Ensure Quality and Reliability 
Improve software quality by ensuring consistent and reliable performance under 
different conditions. 
5. To Reduce Risk 
Minimize the risk of software failure in production, including financial loss and 
reputational damage. 
6. To Improve Performance and Security 
Identify performance bottlenecks and security vulnerabilities. 
7. To Build Customer Confidence 
Deliver a stable and defect-free product, increasing user trust and satisfaction.

Principles of Software Testing 
1. Testing Shows the Presence of Defects 
Testing can identify defects present in the software, but it cannot guarantee the absence of 
defects. Even after extensive testing, some hidden or rare defects may still exist. The goal of 
testing is to reduce risk, not to prove perfection. 
2. Exhaustive Testing is Impossible 
Testing every possible input, output, and path is practically impossible, especially for large 
systems. Therefore, testing must be selective and strategic, focusing on: 
 
Critical functionalities 
 
High-risk areas 
 
Frequently used features 
3. Early Testing Saves Time and Cost 
Testing activities should begin at the earliest stages of software development, such as 
requirement analysis and design. Early detection of defects reduces: 
 
Rework 
 
Development cost 
 
Project delays 
This approach is known as Shift-Left Testing. 
 
4. Defect Clustering 
Defects are not evenly distributed across the system. Typically, a small number of modules 
contain the majority of defects. By identifying and focusing on these defect-prone areas, 
testing becomes more efficient and effective. 
5. Pesticide Paradox 
When the same test cases are executed repeatedly, they eventually stop finding new defects. 
To overcome this: 
 
test cases should be reviewed and updated 
 
new test scenarios should be added 
 
exploratory testing should be performed 
This ensures continuous defect detection. 
6. Testing is Context-Dependent 
There is no one-size-fits-all testing approach. Testing strategies vary depending on: 


--- Page 4 ---

 
type of application (banking, medical, gaming) 
 
level of risk 
 
regulatory requirements 
For example, safety-critical systems require more rigorous testing than casual 
applications. 
7. Absence-of-Errors Fallacy 
Even if software is free from defects, it may still fail if it does not meet user expectations or 
business needs. Therefore, testing must focus not only on correctness but also on usability, 
performance, and relevance.

Fault, Error, Bug, Failure: 
1. Error 
An error is a human mistake made during software development. 
 
Occurs due to misunderstanding requirements, logic mistakes, or poor design 
decisions. 
 
Introduced by developers, analysts, or designers. 
📌 Example: A developer writes if (a > b) instead of if (a >= b). 
2. Fault 
A fault is the incorrect code or logic resulting from an error. 
 
It is a flaw in the program, design, or process. 
 
Also called a defect in some contexts. 
📌 Example: The incorrect condition written in the code. 
3. Bug 
A bug is an informal term used when a fault is discovered during testing. 
 
Commonly used by testers. 
 
Represents unexpected or incorrect behaviour. 
📌 Example: Tester observes wrong output during test execution. 
4. Defect 
A defect is a deviation from expected behaviour or requirements. 


--- Page 5 ---

 
Used in quality and testing documentation. 
 
Can be found during reviews, testing, or inspections. 
📌 Example: Software calculates tax incorrectly compared to requirement document. 
5. Failure 
A failure occurs when the software does not perform its intended function during 
execution. 
 
Visible to end users. 
 
Happens when a fault is executed. 
📌 Example: Application crashes when the user clicks “Submit”. 
 
Error (Human mistake) 
        ↓ 
Fault / Defect (Incorrect code or design) 
        ↓ 
Bug (Fault found during testing) 
        ↓ 
Failure (Observed incorrect behavior at runtime)

1. Requirement Analysis: 
This is the first phase of STLC where testers analyze the software requirements to 
understand what needs to be tested. 
Activities: 
 
Study functional and non-functional requirements 
 
Identify testable and non-testable requirements 
 
Determine testing types required (functional, performance, security, etc.) 
 
Identify risks and testing priorities 
 
Interact with stakeholders for clarification 
Deliverables: 
 
Requirement Traceability Matrix (RTM) 
 
Test feasibility analysis 
Importance: 
Ensures complete test coverage and prevents missing requirements during testing. 
 
2. Test Planning: 
In this phase, a detailed test strategy is prepared that defines how testing will be carried out. 
Activities: 
 
Define testing scope and objectives 
 
Choose testing approach and techniques 
 
Estimate effort, cost, and schedule 
 
Select tools and resources 
 
Identify risks and mitigation plans 
Deliverables: 


--- Page 8 ---

 
Test Plan document 
 
Test Strategy 
 
Resource and effort estimation 
Importance: 
Acts as a blueprint for testing activities. 
 
3. Test Case Design / Development: 
Test cases are designed based on requirements and test strategy. 
Activities: 
 
Write test cases and test scenarios 
 
Prepare test data 
 
Review and update test cases 
 
Develop automation scripts (if applicable) 
Deliverables: 
 
Test cases 
 
Test scripts 
 
Test data 
Importance: 
Ensures systematic and thorough testing of all functionalities. 
4. Test Environment Setup: 
A test environment similar to the production environment is prepared. 
Activities: 
 
Configure hardware and software 
 
Install required tools and applications 
 
Verify environment readiness using smoke tests 
Deliverables: 
 
Test environment setup report 
 
Smoke test results 
 
Importance: 
Ensures testing is done in a realistic and stable environment. 


--- Page 9 ---

5. Test Execution: 
In this phase, test cases are executed and defects are identified. 
Activities: 
 
Execute test cases 
 
Compare actual results with expected results 
 
Log defects with severity and priority 
 
Perform re-testing and regression testing 
 
Track defect status 
Deliverables: 
 
Defect reports 
 
Test execution status 
 
Updated RTM 
Importance: 
Identifies defects and ensures functional correctness of the software. 
6. Test Cycle Closure: 
This is the final phase of STLC where testing activities are formally closed. 
Activities: 
 
Evaluate test completion criteria 
 
Analyze test metrics (defect density, test coverage) 
 
Prepare test summary and closure reports 
 
Document lessons learned 
Deliverables: 
 
Test summary report 
 
Test closure report 
 
Lessons learned document

Software Measurement and Metrics

Software Measurement: A measurement is a manifestation of the size, quantity, 
amount, or dimension of a particular attribute of a product or process. Software 
measurement is a titrate impute of a characteristic of a software product or the 
software process. 
 
Software Measurement Principles 
The software measurement process can be characterized by five activities- 
1. Formulation: The derivation of software measures and metrics appropriate for the 
representation of the software that is being considered. 
2. Collection: The mechanism used to accumulate data required to derive the 
formulated metrics. 
3. Analysis: The computation of metrics and the application of mathematical tools. 
4. Interpretation: The evaluation of metrics results in insight into the quality of the 
representation. 
5. Feedback: Recommendation derived from the interpretation of product metrics 
transmitted to the software team. 
 
Need for Software Measurement 
Software is measured to:  
 Create the quality of the current product or process. 
 Anticipate future qualities of the product or process. 
 Enhance the quality of a product or process. 
 Regulate the state of the project concerning budget and schedule. 
 Enable data-driven decision-making in project planning and control. 
 Identify bottlenecks and areas for improvement to drive process improvement 
activities. 
 Ensure that industry standards and regulations are followed. 
 Give software products and processes a quantitative basis for evaluation. 
 Enable the ongoing improvement of software development practices. 
Classification of Software Measurement 
There are 2 types of software measurement:  
1. Direct Measurement: In direct measurement, the product, process, or thing is 
measured directly using a standard scale. 
2. Indirect Measurement: In indirect measurement, the quantity or quality to be 
measured is measured using related parameters i.e. by use of reference. 
Software Metrics 
A metric is a measurement of the level at which any impute belongs to a system 
product or process.  
Software metrics are a quantifiable or countable assessment of the attributes of a 
software product. There are 4 functions related to software metrics:  


--- Page 12 ---

1. Planning 
2. Organizing 
3. Controlling 
4. Improving 
 
Types of Software Metrics Types of Software Metrics 
1. Product Metrics: Product metrics are used to evaluate the state of the product, 
tracing risks and undercover prospective problem areas. The ability of the team 
to control quality is evaluated. Examples include lines of code, cyclomatic 
complexity, code coverage, defect density, and code maintainability index. 
2. Process Metrics: Process metrics pay particular attention to enhancing the 
long-term process of the team or organization. These metrics are used to 
optimize the development process and maintenance activities of software. 
Examples include effort variance, schedule variance, defect injection rate, and 
lead time. 
3. Project Metrics: The project metrics describes the characteristic and execution 
of a project. Examples include effort estimation accuracy, schedule deviation, 
cost variance, and productivity. Usually measures- 
 
 Number of software developer 
 Staffing patterns over the life cycle of software 
 Cost and schedule 
 Productivity
